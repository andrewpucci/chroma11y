## Design Context

### Users

Chroma11y serves designers, design-system engineers, frontend developers, and accessibility
specialists who need to build accessible color palettes for real products and design systems.
They use the app as a precision tool to generate, inspect, tune, validate, and export palette
systems they can trust in production.

### Brand Personality

Helpful, trustworthy, knowledgeable.

The interface should feel expert without being intimidating. Its tone should communicate clarity,
competence, and practical guidance rather than marketing polish or brand theater. The primary
emotional goal is trustworthiness: users should feel confident that the tool is giving them
credible accessibility guidance and reliable color-system output.

### Aesthetic Direction

The visual direction should remain accessibility-first, technical, and low-brand. Favor clear
structure, legible typography, calm surfaces, and purposeful emphasis over decorative styling.
There is room for visual evolution as long as usability and the product's accessibility goals stay
primary.

Support both light and dark themes. Because branding is intentionally minimal, avoid forcing a
strong signature palette or ornamental visual identity. Use color strategically to clarify state,
contrast, and hierarchy rather than to create a loud brand impression.

There are no formal reference products to imitate. The standard is simply to make this the most
accessible, trustworthy color palette designer possible. Explicit anti-reference: enterprise ERP
software aesthetics.

### Design Principles

1. Accessibility is the product, not a layer. Preserve and strengthen WCAG 2.2 AA support by
   default, with clear paths for users who need to tune toward AAA outcomes.
2. Trust is built through clarity. Favor interfaces that explain complex color decisions clearly,
   expose meaningful controls, and avoid ambiguity in labels, states, and outputs.
3. Function outranks branding. Keep the UI calm, precise, and low-friction; visual expression
   should support comprehension, not compete with it.
4. Design for mixed audiences. The product must work equally well for designers, engineers, and
   accessibility-minded practitioners building systems, not one-off mockups.
5. Evolve intentionally. Visual refinements are welcome, but never at the cost of keyboard
   usability, legibility, contrast communication, or export-oriented workflows.
